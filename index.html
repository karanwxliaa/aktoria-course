Part 1 · Lesson 1

Recipes vs. learning — two kinds of machines

Before robots, before ChatGPT, there is one idea under all of it. It fits in your kitchen.

1. Every machine you grew up with follows a recipe

Think about your washing machine. Somebody in a factory, years ago, wrote its recipe: soak for ten minutes, tumble, rinse, spin for five. Every wash, it performs those exact steps, in that exact order. It has been doing this since the first automatic washing machine appeared in 1937 — and here is the important part: it never once opens the lid to check whether the clothes actually got clean. It follows the recipe; it never tastes the food.

A computer program is exactly this: a recipe card, written by a person, followed by a very obedient cook with no judgment of its own. The ATM, the microwave, the TV remote, the railway ticket machine — all recipe cards. For about seventy years, all software in the world worked this way. If a person could write the steps down, a machine could do them — faster and without ever getting tired.

🍳 Keep this picture

A program is a recipe card. The computer is an extremely obedient cook who does exactly what the card says, the same way every time — and can do nothing that is not on the card.

2. Now try to write the recipe for something easy

Here is where the whole story turns. Some things that feel effortless to you cannot be written down as steps at all. Try it yourself — write the recipe a computer could follow to answer one question: "Is there a dog in this photo?"

🎮 A little game — write the dog rule

Tap a rule you'd write. I'll play the role of the annoyingly honest computer.

Every rule fails. And yet any four-year-old — long before she can read — points at a dog and says "doggy!", even a breed she has never seen, even a dog curled up asleep showing no legs at all. She never learned a rule. She was shown dogs, again and again, until something inside her simply knew.

Scientists have a lovely phrase for this: "we know more than we can tell." You know how to recognize your own child's face in any crowd, at any age, from any angle — but you could not write down how you do it. That knowledge lives in experience, not in words. And no recipe card can hold it.

3. The big switch: stop writing recipes, start showing examples

For about fifty years, very clever programmers tried to make computers see by writing ever-more-clever rules. It barely worked. Computers misread roughly one photo out of every four.

📖 A true story — the woman everyone doubted

About twenty years ago, a young scientist decided everyone had it backwards. The others were competing to write cleverer and cleverer rules — when what was missing, she said, was examples. Teaching a computer to see with a few thousand photos was like raising a child who is allowed only two picture books a year.

People warned her she was wasting her career. Her team spent years building a giant album: 14 million photos, each labeled by hand — "dog," "cup," "bicycle" — by thousands of paid helpers around the world.

Then one team taught a computer not with rules but with over a million of those labeled photos — the way a child is shown dogs. Its mistakes dropped by nearly half in one jump. Within a few years, machines were misreading almost nothing. Every phone camera that finds faces today, every photo app that sorts your pictures, came from that switch.

This new way of building machines is called machine learning: instead of a person writing the recipe, the machine works the recipe out for itself — from thousands or millions of examples. This one idea is behind ChatGPT, behind the robots, and behind your son's company. The examples, not the cleverness, were the missing ingredient.

4. What "training" actually is (no magic, promise)

You will hear the word training constantly around Karan. Here is what it physically means.

Inside the machine sit billions of tiny adjustable numbers — imagine billions of little tuning dials, like on an old radio. Training is a simple loop, repeated a mind-breaking number of times:

guess → check the answer → nudge every dial a hair's width → repeat

The machine looks at an example ("here is a photo — dog or cat?"), makes a guess, is told how wrong it was, and every dial gets turned a tiny amount in the direction that would have made the guess slightly less wrong. One nudge does nothing. Billions upon billions of nudges, guided by correct answers, slowly tune the static into a clear station.

🍲 You already did this

This is exactly how you perfected your own cooking. Nobody handed you the final recipe for your dal thirty years ago. Every pot you ever cooked nudged the salt, the heat, the timing a tiny bit — thousands of dinners, each one a small correction. You were trained by thirty years of examples. The machine does the same thing, just a million times an hour, with none of the taste and all of the patience.

5. And a "model" is… a file

The last word you need from this lesson: after training finishes, all those tuned dials — billions of numbers — get saved. That saved bundle is called a model. It is not a robot, not a machine you could touch. It is a computer file, like a photo or a video is a file, just much bigger — one famous model is a file of about 800 gigabytes, the contents of several full phones.

Two strange and important things about this file:

Nobody wrote it, and nobody can read it. The numbers were found by training, not typed by a person. Even the engineers who built the system cannot open the file and point to where "the capital of France" is stored — the knowledge is smeared across all the dials at once, the way your feel for perfect roti dough is smeared across thirty years of hands, not written in any notebook.

It can be copied. Your thirty years of kitchen instinct cannot be handed to anyone — your daughter-in-law has to learn it the slow way. A model is that same kind of practiced instinct, except it can be photocopied to a million phones overnight. This slightly unfair superpower — experience that can be photocopied — is why the whole world is racing to build these files. And the same robot arm, the same chat app, is clumsy or brilliant depending on which model file is loaded into it — like the same phone being a different tool depending on the app.

Part 1 · Lesson 2

ChatGPT — the sentence-finishing machine

The famous talking computer, explained with something that's been on your phone all along.

1. You already use a tiny ChatGPT every day

Open WhatsApp and start typing. Above the keyboard, three little word suggestions appear. Type "Happy" and your phone offers "birthday". It learned this from seeing what people (including you) usually type next. It is playing a guessing game: given the words so far, what word probably comes next?

Hold that thought, because here is the secret of the most famous machine in the world: ChatGPT plays exactly the same game. That's the whole trick. Given everything written so far in the conversation, it predicts one likely next word. Writes it. Then predicts the next. And the next — one word at a time, hundreds of times — until an answer appears. It is not searching the internet for an answer. It is not copying from a stored book. It is finishing the sentence, the way you finish a proverb everyone knows.

🎮 Play the game ChatGPT plays

Build a sentence by tapping the word you think comes next — just like your keyboard strip.

The difference between your keyboard and ChatGPT is not the game — it's the size of the player. Your keyboard's little guesser looks at your last word or two. ChatGPT's guesser can consider about 300 pages of conversation at once, and it runs on hundreds of billions of those tuning dials from Lesson 1, in a giant computer building. Same idea — bicycle versus jumbo jet.

2. What did it read to get this good?

Remember the rule from Lesson 1: the student is only as good as its examples. So — how many examples of human writing did these machines study? Sit down for this.

Where did that mountain of text come from? The internet — essentially for free. Humanity spent thirty years writing emails, articles, recipes, arguments, books, reviews... and it was all just lying there. Nobody had to be paid to create the study material. Remember this fact. In Lesson 3 it becomes the key to Karan's entire industry.

(One small word you may hear Karan say: "tokens." Machines count text in tokens — word-pieces. Four tokens is roughly three words. That's all it means.)

And here's a comforting thought. A child becomes fluent in her language after hearing fewer than 100 million words. The machine needed trillions — tens of thousands of times more. So no, the machine is not a genius child. It is a spectacularly slow student with infinite stamina, making up in volume what it lacks in your grandchildren's spark.

3. Why it makes mistakes — confidently

📖 A true story — the lawyer who didn't check

A few years ago, a lawyer in New York asked ChatGPT to find court cases for a lawsuit against an airline. It gave him six perfect-looking cases — names, judges, quotes. He submitted them to the court.

All six were invented. ChatGPT hadn't "lied" — it had done its one and only job: compose text that looks like what lawyers write. The judge fined him $5,000, and the story went around the world.

Why does a machine this capable invent things? Because fluency and truth come out of the same guessing machinery. A wrong answer arrives in exactly the same polished, confident tone as a right one — so you cannot judge it by tone, the way you'd judge a smooth-talking wedding guest. The people who build these machines explain it with a picture any mother of students will recognize: the machine is like an exam-taker who knows that a blank answer scores zero but a confident guess might score — so it learned to never leave a question blank, and never say "I don't know."

When testers quizzed chatbots on tricky law questions, the chatbots got more than half wrong — but versions that could first look things up in real law books made far fewer mistakes. Notice what fixed it: better material to lean on. Always the data.

So the family rule is: use it freely, check what matters. It is a brilliant, tireless, occasionally-wrong assistant — wonderful for drafts, explanations, translations, ideas; and anything important you verify, like you'd taste a new cook's dish before serving it to guests.

4. One more secret: humans taught it manners

After all that reading, the machine could talk — but not behave. So companies paid thousands of people to chat with it and grade its answers: thumbs up for helpful and polite, thumbs down for rude or wrong. Dial-nudging, again — but with paid human teachers instead of free internet text. Hold onto this too: behind every "smart" machine stands an army of paid human teachers. In the robot world, some of those teachers work for your son.

One last twist: the world is actually running out of fresh human writing to feed these machines — researchers estimate the useful public text of the internet will be essentially all used up within a few years. Good study material has become one of the most precious things in technology. Keep that thought warm; it comes back with force in Lesson 6.

Part 2 · Lesson 3

Why robots are hard — and the empty library

Computers beat chess champions thirty years ago. So why is there still no robot folding your laundry? The answer is the key to everything Karan does.

1. What a robot actually is

A robot is three things joined together:

A body — motors, metal arms, wheels, hands.
Senses — cameras for eyes, sometimes microphones, very rarely any sense of touch.
A brain — the part that decides what to move next.

Here is the surprise: the body was solved long ago. Factory robots have existed since the 1960s. Today about 4.7 million of them work in the world's factories. Strong, tireless, precise bodies are old news. The battle of our time is over the brain — and, as you'll see, over the brain's study material.

2. The robots you've already seen are not what you think

You've seen factory robots on TV — rows of orange arms welding cars in showers of sparks. They look intelligent. They are not. They are precise. A welding robot repeats one recorded motion, accurate to a hair's width, a million times — but most of them are completely blind. No cameras, no touch. If the car arrived a few centimetres out of place, the robot would confidently weld the empty air. It's a music box: it plays its one tune perfectly, and if you nudge the piano aside, it keeps "playing" in the air.

That's why factory robots stand behind safety cages — not because they're dangerous minds, but because they're blind muscles. A person could stand right next to one and it would never know.

And the "robots" in your own home? The washing machine follows its fixed recipe and never checks the clothes. The robot vacuum cleans the way a blindfolded person would sweep: roll forward, bump the sofa, turn, roll again. Millions have been sold — and after twenty years, not one of them has ever picked up a single sock. It has no hands, and nothing it does today makes it better tomorrow. None of these machines learn.

3. The upside-down rule

Now, the strangest and most important fact in this whole school:

🙃 The upside-down rule

What is hard for people is easy for computers. What is so easy for people that we don't even call it a skill — is nearly impossible for robots.

A computer beat the world chess champion back in 1997. But here's the part of that story nobody remembers: the computer couldn't move its own pieces. An engineer sat beside the board, moving each pawn for it. The machine out-thought the greatest chess mind alive — and couldn't do what a two-year-old does with her fingers.

Think of a brilliant professor who calculates the whole family's taxes in his head in one minute — but cannot crack an egg, button his shirt, or pour tea without spilling. Computers are that professor. Why? Because chess and arithmetic are recent human party tricks, but hands, eyes and balance were polished over millions of years. The things that feel effortless — picking up a coin, folding a shirt — are actually our deepest, oldest, most refined skills. We just never noticed, because everyone has them.

Picking up a grape with a robot gripper is like picking it up with barbecue tongs, wearing thick oven mitts, with your eyes closed. And soft, floppy things are the boss level: a brick behaves the same every time you grab it, but a shirt is a different shape every second you touch it — like shaking hands with water. That's why a towel once defeated a robot that cost as much as a house.

Even the world's biggest online shop, with over a million robots in its warehouses, had them mostly carrying shelves around for a decade — humans kept doing the grabbing, because grabbing a toothpaste tube, a glass jar, a floppy bag was too hard for machines. Their first robot arm that can actually feel what it touches arrived only last year.

4. The empty library — the sentence this whole school stands on

Now bring back the golden rule from Lessons 1 and 2: machines get smart from examples. ChatGPT got smart because its study material — all the text humanity ever typed — was already lying on the internet, free. It walked into a full library.

So what happens when a robot goes looking for its study material? It needs examples of a very particular kind: exactly how do fingers move when folding a shirt? How hard do you grip a cup? At what angle does the wrist turn when pouring?

And the shelf is empty.

Nobody, in all of human history, ever wrote that down. There is no WhatsApp of hand movements. No YouTube of grip strength. Billions of people fold laundry every day — and not one of those movements was ever recorded in a form a robot can learn from. The talking machines inherited a full library for free; the robots' library has to be written now, by hand, page by page — and someone has to pay the writers.

🔑 The hinge of everything

ChatGPT arrived overnight because its textbook already existed. Robot helpers are arriving slowly because their textbook is being written right now — one recorded demonstration at a time. Writing that missing library is the industry your son works in. Everything in the next lessons — the remote driving, the recorded lessons, the special tools — is about writing it faster.

How big is the gap? The talking machines studied the equivalent of roughly a hundred thousand years of human reading. All the robot "lessons" collected in the entire world so far add up to about one year of experience. That gap — a hundred thousand to one — is the single biggest business opportunity in technology today. Hold that number; in Lesson 6 you'll meet the people filling it.

Check yourself

Factory robots weld cars perfectly. So why can't they make a cup of tea?

Because they don't understand anything — they replay one recorded motion in a world engineered to never change. Tea lives in your kitchen, where nothing is ever in the same place twice. Precision is not intelligence.

What's the upside-down rule?

Hard-for-humans (chess, taxes) is easy for computers; easy-for-humans (picking up a grape, folding a towel) is the hardest thing in the world for robots — because hands and eyes are our oldest, most polished skills.

Why did talking machines arrive before robot helpers?

The talking machines found their study material — the internet's text — already written, free. The robots' study material (recorded hand movements) never existed. It has to be created from scratch, by people, one demonstration at a time. That's the empty library.

Part 2 · Lesson 4

Driving a robot from far away

The word Karan says most: teleoperation. It sounds technical. It's actually a video call — where your hands come out the other side.

1. A video call with hands

On a WhatsApp video call you can see Karan's kitchen from across the world — but you can't stir his pot. Now imagine a robot standing in that kitchen. On your screen you see through its camera eyes. You move your hands — and its hands copy you, the same instant. You could stir the pot from another city. That is teleoperation: seeing through a robot's eyes and lending it your hands, over the internet.

How does the robot know what your hands are doing? Two common ways:

Twin arms. The operator holds a small, light "leader" arm. The robot's arm copies every motion exactly — like teaching a child to write by holding their hand over the pencil, except the child's hand is in another building.

Wearing the robot. The operator puts on a headset showing the robot's camera view, and holds motion controllers. It's the puppeteer moved inside the puppet — no strings, just the internet.

2. This is older and more trusted than you think

Teleoperation wasn't invented for fun. It was invented in the 1940s so scientists could handle radioactive material: behind thick protective glass, a person moved two handles, and metal tongs on the dangerous side copied every motion. Human skill, sent through a wall.

And today? You may have heard of "robot surgery." Here is what those words really mean: the surgeon sits at a console a few steps from the patient, looking into a 3D viewer, moving finger controls — and robot arms turn those movements into tiny, perfectly steady movements inside the patient. The robot never decides anything. Every single motion is the surgeon's. Millions of operations are done this way every year — families trust a teleoperated robot with life itself, usually without knowing the word for it.

Even better: the robot makes the surgeon's hands superhuman. Big comfortable hand movements become tiny stitches — and the natural little tremble every human hand has is electronically smoothed away, like pouring tea at your own table instead of on a moving train.

📖 A true story — the operation across the ocean

One day in 2001, a surgeon sat down at a console in New York. His patient — a 68-year-old woman — lay on an operating table in France, an ocean away. Through a special high-speed line, he removed her gallbladder in 45 minutes, his hand movements crossing the Atlantic and coming back as video. She recovered perfectly.

The total delay — hand moves, robot moves, picture returns — was about one-sixth of a second. About one blink of an eye. Remember that number; it's about to matter.

3. The enemy: delay

Here is the whole engineering battle of Karan's company, in one word: delay (engineers say "latency"). It's the time between your movement and seeing the robot respond.

You have already felt it. That awkward moment on a video call when you both start talking, stop, and say "no no, you go first" — that's delay. Annoying in conversation. Deadly for hands. Imagine heating milk while only watching the pot through a TV broadcast that runs one second behind: by the time your screen shows the milk rising, it has already boiled over on the real stove. Your hands are always acting on old news.

🎮 Feel the delay yourself

Drag your finger (or mouse) in the box. The orange dot is the robot's hand trying to follow you. Try each button — and imagine threading a needle.

With Karan's software, the dot feels glued to your finger — the robot moves before a blink finishes.

The rough rule engineers discovered: up to about one-fifth of a second of delay, remote hands feel almost natural. Beyond that, every movement becomes slow, careful, tiring — and delicate work becomes impossible. That's why each level of delay decides what kind of remote work can be done:

0.03 s

0.15 s

1.5 s

13 min

Now you can read the proudest line on your son's website like an insider. It says their delay is under 8 thousandths of a second nearby, and about 30–40 across the entire country. A blink takes 100–150. In plain words: an operator in one city can lend her hands to a robot in another city, and the robot's hands feel glued to hers. Distance has stopped being the problem. That is genuinely hard to build — and it's the foundation everything else in the company stands on.

Reason one: work gets done today. A robot that can't manage alone can still be useful right now, with a human's skill flowing into it from far away.

Reason two — and this is the beautiful part: remember the empty library from Lesson 3? Every minute of teleoperation is recorded — what the cameras saw, plus every movement of every joint. Each recording is one page written into the robots' missing library. The same hour of work is sold twice: the customer gets the job done, and the recording becomes a lesson that teaches robots to do it alone next time.

A hired cook who makes tonight's dinner and leaves behind a perfect video recipe after every meal — that's teleoperation. Lessons 5 and 6 are these two reasons, one each. You now hold the master key to the whole business.

Part 3 · Lesson 5

The humans behind every robot

The robot industry's open secret — and why it's not a scandal, but a plan. This lesson is the first half of what Aktoria sells.

1. The open secret

Karan's company introduces itself with one cheeky sentence: "Every 'autonomous' robot you've seen this year had a human behind it."

They mean it literally. Those jaw-dropping robot videos online — a humanoid loading a dishwasher, pouring a drink, tidying a room? As a rule of thumb: if a robot did something delicate and surprising, a person was probably driving it — from the next room or the next continent, exactly the way you learned in Lesson 4.

📖 A true story — the $20,000 house robot

Last year, a company started selling the first real humanoid robot for homes — a soft, friendly machine in a knitted suit, about the height of a person, for $20,000. A famous tech journalist invited one into a house to test it.

Fetching a bottle of water took over a minute. Loading a few dishes took five. And then came the discovery that made headlines: every single thing the robot did was being driven, live, by a company employee wearing a headset in another building. The internet had a good laugh — "$20,000 to let a stranger wander my house!"

But the company never denied it. That is the plan, they said, openly: the human drivers are the robot's teachers. Owners schedule a session, a trained operator drives the robot through the chore — and every session is recorded and used to teach the robot to do it alone next time. It IS the robot age — the stage of it we're living in now.

2. Why robots need a human safety net

Here's the business logic, and it's very simple. Suppose a robot does a task correctly nine times out of ten. Sounds good — but no shop, hospital or warehouse can accept a worker who fails every tenth time and then just… stands there, stuck. That one-in-ten is the wall between "impressive demo" and "actually useful."

The fix: when the robot gets stuck, a trained human takes over remotely, sorts it out in a minute, and hands control back. Suddenly the robot's work is dependable today — years before the robot itself is perfect. The human is the safety net that lets robots leave the laboratory and start working in the real world now.

And remember the gold from Lesson 4: every one of those rescues is recorded. The robot got saved — and the library got one more page about exactly the situations robots find hardest. The safety net is also the school.

Robot lessons — the treasure everyone is buying

The second half of what Aktoria sells: the recorded lessons themselves. Time to open one up and look inside.

1. What one robot lesson actually is

The industry's word for one recorded lesson is an "episode." When Karan says "we delivered a batch of episodes," here is what he means.

Imagine filming yourself making one roti — but with a helper who also writes down, fifty times every second, the exact position of your shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers, and how hard you're pinching the dough. The video plus that notebook of numbers, for one single roti, start to finish — that bundle is one episode.

🔬 Look inside a live episode

This is what the robot's memory of ten seconds of work looks like — several cameras at once, plus the numbers, written down 50 times every second:

🫖overhead camera
🤖front camera
✋left wrist camera
🤚right wrist camera
elbow 42.1° · wrist 118.3° · grip 0.82 · reach 31.4 cm
snapshot #0 of this episode

Notice the wrist cameras — the robot films its own hands close-up, because during delicate work its arms block the main camera's view. A movie shows 24 pictures per second; an episode writes everything down 50 times per second — finer than cinema, because a hand pouring tea moves too much between two coarse snapshots.

This also answers a very sensible question: "Why can't robots just watch cooking videos on YouTube?" Because an ordinary video shows what happened — but not the angles, distances and grip pressure. The robot needs the notebook, not just the film. That's why special recording tools exist… and why this data can't simply be scraped off the internet. It has to be made.

Those fresh episodes, ordered again and again, are exactly the repeat business a data company lives on.

3. The two rules of good lessons

Rule one: variety. A cook who has only ever cooked in her own kitchen panics in her sister's kitchen — different stove, different drawers, dimmer light. A cook who has cooked in fifty kitchens can cook anywhere. Robots are the first kind of cook unless you deliberately show them many kitchens. So collectors keep moving the cup, changing the tablecloth, dimming the lights — fifty slightly different lessons teach the robot to cope; fifty identical ones only teach it to repeat.

Rule two: quality. A photocopier copies the smudges along with the letters — and a small child copies not just your words but your accent. The robot is both: if the demonstrator hesitates, fumbles, or takes a lazy shortcut, the robot faithfully learns to hesitate, fumble and shortcut. It was even measured: 200 lessons from one calm expert taught a better robot than 300 lessons from six careless helpers. Now you know why Aktoria insists on trained, in-house operators instead of random freelancers — the robot photocopies its teacher.

("Can't they practice inside a computer game instead?" They do — some robot hands have practiced for centuries inside simulations. But dreams are never quite like the real pitch: the real cloth is stickier, the real light glares. Dream-practice helps; real human-guided lessons on real objects remain the gold.)

4. The three recording tools — Aktoria sells all three

Tool 1: The robot itself, driven by a human — everything from Lesson 4. The gold standard, because the lesson happened in the robot's own body: its cameras, its joints, its grip. Slower and more expensive — you need a robot and a trained operator — but nothing beats it.

Tool 2: The magic tongs. A clever handheld tool: a robot's gripper you hold in your hand, with a small camera on top. No robot in the room at all — a person just does the chore holding it, squeezing it like kitchen tongs, and the camera plus a motion sensor record everything a robot will later need. It costs like a phone instead of like a car, and one person collects about three times more lessons per hour than by driving a robot. Each lesson is literally one video file. Robots trained purely on these hand-tool lessons have washed dishes and folded sweaters.

Tool 3: Camera glasses. An ordinary-looking pair of glasses with two little cameras where your two eyes are — two, because that's how depth is judged (close one eye and try pouring water into a far-away glass!) — plus a motion sensor like the inner ear that tells you you're turning even with eyes shut. A person simply wears them and lives: cooking, folding, tidying. The robot later relives the day through human eyes. This is the cheapest lesson-maker of all — and the big realization behind it is lovely: humanity performs billions of hours of hand-work every day; glasses can finally harvest it. Even mixing just 90 minutes of glasses-recordings into a robot's training has made it dramatically better at those chores.

On Karan's website these are the three data products: robot episodes, handheld-gripper episodes, and "stereo ego" — which you can now decode like an engineer: stereo = two eyes, ego = filmed from the doer's own point of view.
