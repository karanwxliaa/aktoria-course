Part 1 · Lesson 1

Recipes vs. learning — two kinds of machines

Before robots, before ChatGPT, there is one idea under all of it. It fits in your kitchen.

1. Every machine you grew up with follows a recipe

Think about your washing machine. Someone in a factory wrote its recipe once: soak, tumble, rinse, spin. Every wash, it does those exact steps — and it never opens the lid to check if the clothes actually got clean. It follows the recipe; it never tastes the food.

A computer program is exactly this: a recipe card written by a person, followed by a very obedient cook with no judgment. The ATM, the microwave, the ticket machine — all recipe cards. For seventy years, all software worked this way.

🍳 Keep this picture

A program is a recipe card. The computer is an extremely obedient cook who does exactly what the card says, the same way every time — and can do nothing that is not on the card.

2. Now try writing the recipe for something "easy"

Here's where the story turns. Some things that feel effortless cannot be written as steps at all. Try it — what recipe tells a computer: "Is there a dog in this photo?"

🎮 A little game — write the dog rule

Tap a rule you'd write. I'll play the role of the annoyingly honest computer.

Every rule fails. Yet any four-year-old points at a dog and says "doggy!" — even a breed she's never seen. She never learned a rule. She was shown dogs, again and again, until she simply knew.

You're the same: you'd spot your own child in any crowd, at any age — but you couldn't write down how. Some knowledge lives in experience, not words. No recipe card can hold it.

3. The big switch: stop writing recipes, start showing examples

For fifty years, clever programmers tried to make computers see by writing cleverer rules. It barely worked — computers misread one photo in four.

📖 A true story — the woman everyone doubted

About twenty years ago, a young scientist said everyone had it backwards: what was missing wasn't cleverer rules, it was examples. People warned she was wasting her career. Her team spent years collecting 14 million photos, each labeled by hand — "dog," "cup," "bicycle."

Then a computer was taught with a million of those photos instead of rules — the way a child is shown dogs. Its mistakes dropped by nearly half in one jump. Within a few years, machines misread almost nothing. Every phone camera that finds faces today came from that switch.

This is machine learning: the machine works out its own recipe from many examples. This one idea is behind ChatGPT, behind robots, and behind your son's company. The examples, not the cleverness, were the missing ingredient.

4. What "training" actually is (no magic, promise)

You'll hear the word training constantly around Karan. Here's what it really means.

Inside the machine sit billions of tiny tuning dials, like on an old radio. Training is one simple loop, repeated endlessly:

guess → check the answer → nudge every dial a hair's width → repeat

One nudge does nothing. Billions of nudges, guided by correct answers, slowly tune the static into a clear station.

🍲 You already did this

It's how you perfected your own cooking. Every pot in thirty years nudged the salt, the heat, the timing a tiny bit. You were trained by thousands of dinners. The machine does the same — a million times an hour, with no taste and endless patience.

5. And a "model" is… a file

When training finishes, all those tuned dials get saved. That saved bundle is a model. Not a robot, not a machine — a computer file, like a photo or video, just much bigger.

Two strange things about this file:

Nobody wrote it, and nobody can read it. Even its makers can't open it and point to where "the capital of France" is stored — the knowledge is smeared across all the dials, the way your feel for perfect dough is smeared across thirty years of hands, written in no notebook.

It can be copied. Your kitchen instinct can't be handed to anyone — a model is that same practiced instinct, but photocopyable to a million phones overnight. And the same robot arm is clumsy or brilliant depending on which model file is loaded into it — like the same phone with a different app.

ChatGPT — the sentence-finishing machine

The famous talking computer, explained with something that's been on your phone all along.

1. You already use a tiny ChatGPT every day

Start typing in WhatsApp. Above the keyboard, three word suggestions appear. Type "Happy" and your phone offers "birthday". It's playing a guessing game: given the words so far, what probably comes next?

Here's the secret of the most famous machine in the world: ChatGPT plays exactly the same game. It predicts one likely next word, writes it, then predicts the next — hundreds of times — until an answer appears. It's not searching the internet. It's not copying from a book. It's finishing the sentence, the way you finish a proverb everyone knows.

🎮 Play the game ChatGPT plays

Build a sentence by tapping the word you think comes next — just like your keyboard strip.

The difference isn't the game — it's the size of the player. Your keyboard looks at your last word or two. ChatGPT considers about 300 pages at once, using billions of those tuning dials from Lesson 1. Same idea — bicycle versus jumbo jet.

2. What did it read to get this good?

The student is only as good as its examples. So how much human writing did these machines study? Sit down for this.

Where did that mountain of text come from? The internet — for free. Humanity spent thirty years writing emails, recipes, articles, arguments… and it was all just lying there. Remember this. In Lesson 3 it becomes the key to Karan's entire industry.

A comforting thought: a child becomes fluent after hearing under 100 million words. The machine needed trillions. It's not a genius child — it's a very slow student with infinite stamina, making up in volume what it lacks in your grandchildren's spark.

3. Why it makes mistakes — confidently

📖 A true story — the lawyer who didn't check

A lawyer in New York asked ChatGPT to find court cases for a lawsuit. It gave him six perfect-looking cases — names, judges, quotes. He submitted them to the court.

All six were invented. ChatGPT hadn't "lied" — it did its only job: compose text that looks like what lawyers write. The judge fined him $5,000.

Why does it invent things? Because right and wrong answers come out of the same guessing machinery, in the same polished tone — you can't judge it by confidence, like a smooth-talking wedding guest. It's an exam-taker who knows a blank answer scores zero but a confident guess might score — so it never says "I don't know."

So the family rule: use it freely, check what matters. A brilliant, tireless, occasionally-wrong assistant. Wonderful for drafts, explanations, translations — and anything important, you verify, like tasting a new cook's dish before serving guests.

4. One more secret: humans taught it manners

After all that reading, the machine could talk — but not behave. So companies paid thousands of people to grade its answers: thumbs up for helpful, thumbs down for rude or wrong. Behind every "smart" machine stands an army of paid human teachers. In the robot world, some of those teachers work for your son.

One last twist: the world is running out of fresh writing to feed these machines — the internet's useful text will be mostly used up within a few years. Good study material has become one of the most precious things in technology. Keep that warm; it returns with force in Lesson 6.

Part 2 · Lesson 3

Why robots are hard — and the empty library

Computers beat chess champions thirty years ago. So why is there still no robot folding your laundry? The answer is the key to everything Karan does.

1. What a robot actually is

A robot is three things joined together:

A body — motors, metal arms, wheels, hands.
Senses — cameras for eyes, sometimes microphones, very rarely any sense of touch.
A brain — the part that decides what to move next.

The surprise: the body was solved long ago. Factory robots have existed since the 1960s — about 4.7 million work in factories today. The battle of our time is over the brain — and over the brain's study material.

2. The robots you've seen are not what you think

Factory robots welding cars in showers of sparks look intelligent. They're not — they're precise. A welding robot repeats one recorded motion a million times, but most are completely blind: no cameras, no touch. If the car arrived a bit out of place, it would confidently weld the empty air. That's why they stand behind safety cages — not dangerous minds, just blind muscles.

And at home? The robot vacuum cleans like a blindfolded person sweeping: roll, bump the sofa, turn, roll again. Twenty years, millions sold — and it has never picked up a single sock. None of these machines learn.

3. The upside-down rule

Now, the strangest and most important fact in this whole school:

🙃 The upside-down rule

What is hard for people is easy for computers. What is so easy for people that we don't even call it a skill — is nearly impossible for robots.

A computer beat the world chess champion back in 1997. The part nobody remembers: it couldn't move its own pieces. An engineer sat beside the board, moving each pawn for it. It out-thought the greatest chess mind alive — and couldn't do what a two-year-old does with her fingers.

Computers are a brilliant professor who calculates the family's taxes in his head — but can't crack an egg or pour tea without spilling. Why? Chess and arithmetic are recent party tricks. Hands, eyes and balance were polished over millions of years. The things that feel effortless — picking up a coin, folding a shirt — are our deepest, oldest skills. We never noticed, because everyone has them.

And soft things are the boss level: a brick behaves the same every time, but a shirt changes shape every second you touch it — like shaking hands with water. A towel once defeated a robot that cost as much as a house.

Even the world's biggest online shop, with a million warehouse robots, had them mostly carrying shelves for a decade — humans kept doing the grabbing. Their first robot arm that can actually feel what it touches arrived only last year.

4. The empty library — the sentence this whole school stands on

Remember the golden rule: machines get smart from examples. ChatGPT's study material — all the text humanity ever typed — was already on the internet, free. It walked into a full library.

Now the robot goes looking for its study material: how exactly do fingers move when folding a shirt? How hard do you grip a cup?

And the shelf is empty.

Nobody, in all of history, ever wrote that down. There is no WhatsApp of hand movements, no YouTube of grip strength. Billions of people fold laundry daily — and not one movement was recorded in a form a robot can learn from. The robots' library has to be written now, page by page — and someone has to pay the writers.

🔑 The hinge of everything

ChatGPT arrived overnight because its textbook already existed. Robot helpers are arriving slowly because their textbook is being written right now — one recorded demonstration at a time. Writing that missing library is the industry your son works in.

How big is the gap? The talking machines studied the equivalent of about a hundred thousand years of reading. Part 2 · Lesson 4

Driving a robot from far away

The word Karan says most: teleoperation. It sounds technical. It's actually a video call — where your hands come out the other side.

1. A video call with hands

On a WhatsApp video call you can see Karan's kitchen — but you can't stir his pot. Now imagine a robot standing in that kitchen. You see through its camera eyes, you move your hands — and its hands copy you, the same instant. That is teleoperation: lending a robot your hands, over the internet.

How does it know what your hands are doing? Two ways: twin arms — the operator holds a small "leader" arm and the robot's arm copies it, like guiding a child's hand over the pencil from another building. Or wearing the robot — a headset shows the robot's view, controllers carry your movements. The puppeteer, moved inside the puppet.

2. Older and more trusted than you think

Teleoperation was invented in the 1940s so scientists could handle radioactive material: behind thick glass, a person moved two handles, and metal tongs on the dangerous side copied every motion.

And "robot surgery" today? The surgeon sits at a console, moving finger controls — robot arms turn them into tiny, perfectly steady movements inside the patient. The robot never decides anything; every motion is the surgeon's. Millions of operations a year happen this way. Even better: big comfortable hand movements become tiny stitches, and the natural tremble of every human hand is smoothed away.

📖 A true story — the operation across the ocean

In 2001, a surgeon sat at a console in New York. His patient lay on a table in France, an ocean away. Through a special high-speed line, he removed her gallbladder in 45 minutes. She recovered perfectly.

The delay — hand moves, robot moves, picture returns — was about one blink of an eye. Remember that; it's about to matter.

3. The enemy: delay

Karan's whole engineering battle in one word: delay (engineers say "latency") — the time between your movement and seeing the robot respond.

You've felt it: that awkward video-call moment when you both start talking, stop, "no no, you go first." Annoying in conversation. Deadly for hands. Imagine heating milk while watching the pot through a broadcast running one second behind — by the time you see the milk rising, it already boiled over. Your hands are acting on old news.

🎮 Feel the delay yourself

Drag your finger (or mouse) in the box. The orange dot is the robot's hand trying to follow you. Try each button — and imagine threading a needle.

With Karan's software, the dot feels glued to your finger — the robot moves before a blink finishes.

The rule engineers discovered: up to about one-fifth of a second, remote hands feel almost natural. Beyond that, delicate work falls apart. Each level of delay decides what kind of remote work is possible:

Now you can read the proudest line on your son's website like an insider: delay under 8 thousandths of a second nearby, about 30–40 across a whole country. A blink takes 100–150. In plain words: an operator in one city lends her hands to a robot in another, and the robot's hands feel glued to hers. That is genuinely hard to build — and everything else stands on it.

Part 3 · Lesson 5

The humans behind every robot

The robot industry's open secret — and why it's not a scandal, but a plan. This lesson is the first half of what Aktoria sells.

1. The open secret

Karan's company introduces itself with one cheeky sentence: "Every 'autonomous' robot you've seen this year had a human behind it."

They mean it literally. Those jaw-dropping robot videos online? Rule of thumb: if a robot did something delicate and surprising, a person was probably driving it — from the next room or the next continent.

📖 A true story — the $20,000 house robot

Last year, a company started selling the first humanoid robot for homes — a friendly machine in a knitted suit, for $20,000. A famous journalist tested one. Fetching a water bottle took over a minute. And then the discovery: everything the robot did was being driven, live, by an employee in a headset in another building. The internet laughed — "$20,000 to let a stranger wander my house!"

But the company never denied it. That is the plan: the human drivers are the robot's teachers. Every session is recorded and teaches the robot to do the chore alone next time. Early buyers aren't buying a finished servant — they're hiring an apprentice, plus the tutor.

Once you see the pattern, it's everywhere. Driverless taxis? A team of about seventy trained helpers sits in control rooms, answering cars' questions within seconds when something confuses them. Campus delivery robots? Watched for years by people in another country. Humans-in-the-loop isn't a failure of the robot age. It IS the robot age — the stage we're living in now.

2. Why robots need a human safety net

The business logic is simple. Say a robot does a task right nine times out of ten. Sounds good — but no shop or warehouse can accept a worker who fails every tenth time and then just stands there, stuck. That one-in-ten is the wall between "impressive demo" and "actually useful."

The fix: when the robot gets stuck, a trained human takes over remotely, sorts it out in a minute, hands control back. The robot's work becomes dependable today — years before the robot is perfect. And every rescue is recorded: one more page for the library, about exactly the situations robots find hardest. The safety net is also the school.

3. A call center for hands

Who provides these safety nets? A robot company could hire its own operators… in every time zone… covering nights and sick days… That's a whole second company to run. You know how the world solves this: when you call your bank at 3 a.m., a trained person answers — banks share professional call centers instead of each building their own.

☎️ The picture to keep

Aktoria runs a call center for hands. Rows of trained people on shift, day and night — but instead of answering phones, each seat is driving a robot somewhere in the world. A robot gets stuck at 3 a.m. in an American warehouse? It "rings" the operations floor, a trained operator takes its hands for a few minutes, the job continues. The customer's robots get round-the-clock human skill — without the customer hiring a single operator.

The clever part is the clock: operators in India, Mexico and the USA — when America sleeps, India is wide awake. Three countries passing the shift around the globe like a family shop that never closes. And they're trained, in-house staff, because who does the driving matters enormously (Lesson 6 shows why).

"Robot operator" is a brand-new profession, being invented right now — skilled work, teaching machines with your hands. Your son isn't just running a company; he's helping invent a job.

Part 3 · Lesson 6

Robot lessons — the treasure everyone is buying

The second half of what Aktoria sells: the recorded lessons themselves. Time to open one up and look inside.

1. What one robot lesson actually is

The industry's word for one recorded lesson is an "episode." When Karan says "we delivered a batch of episodes," here's what he means.

Imagine filming yourself making one roti — while a helper also writes down, fifty times every second, the exact position of your shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers, and how hard you're pinching the dough. The video plus that notebook of numbers, for one roti, start to finish — that's one episode.

🔬 Look inside a live episode

This is what the robot's memory of ten seconds of work looks like — several cameras at once, plus the numbers, written down 50 times every second:

🫖overhead camera
🤖front camera
✋left wrist camera
🤚right wrist camera
elbow 42.1° · wrist 118.3° · grip 0.82 · reach 31.4 cm
snapshot #0 of this episode

A movie shows 24 pictures per second; an episode writes everything down 50 times per second — finer than cinema, because a pouring hand moves too much between two coarse snapshots.

This answers a sensible question: "Why can't robots just watch YouTube cooking videos?" Because ordinary video shows what happened — not the angles, distances and grip pressure. The robot needs the notebook, not just the film. This data can't be scraped off the internet. It has to be made.

2. How many lessons does a robot need?

~50
demonstrations to teach one chore. A famous student-built robot learned to cook shrimp from about 50 guided lessons
10,000 hours
of episodes behind one of today's best general robot brains — and that's barely ONE year of experience
1,000,000
episodes when the world's labs once pooled everything they had. The chatbots' library is still ~100,000× bigger

See the shape? One chore needs about fifty lessons — you wouldn't let someone drive alone after one lesson either. A general robot brain needs thousands of hours. And once one exists, each new chore takes just a few hours of fresh episodes — like an experienced cook seeing a new dish once or twice. Those fresh episodes are the repeat business a data company lives on.

3. The two rules of good lessons

Rule one: variety. A cook who has only cooked in her own kitchen panics in her sister's. A cook who has cooked in fifty kitchens can cook anywhere. So collectors keep moving the cup, changing the tablecloth, dimming the lights — fifty slightly different lessons teach the robot to cope; fifty identical ones only teach it to repeat.

Rule two: quality. A photocopier copies the smudges along with the letters. If the demonstrator hesitates, fumbles or takes shortcuts, the robot faithfully learns to hesitate, fumble and shortcut. It's been measured: 200 lessons from one calm expert beat 300 from six careless helpers. Now you know why Aktoria insists on trained, in-house operators — the robot photocopies its teacher.

("Can't they practice inside a computer game?" They do. But dreams are never quite the real pitch — the real cloth is stickier, the real light glares. Real human-guided lessons on real objects remain the gold.)

4. The three recording tools — Aktoria sells all three

Tool 1: The robot itself, driven by a human — Lesson 4's teleoperation. The gold standard, because the lesson happened in the robot's own body: its cameras, its joints, its grip. Slower and more expensive, but nothing beats it.

Tool 2: The magic tongs. A robot's gripper you hold in your hand, with a small camera on top. No robot in the room — a person just does the chore holding it, and the camera plus motion sensor record everything a robot will later need. Costs like a phone instead of a car, and collects about three times more lessons per hour. Robots trained purely on these have washed dishes and folded sweaters.

Tool 3: Camera glasses. Glasses with two little cameras where your eyes are — two, because that's how depth is judged (close one eye and try pouring into a far glass!) — plus a motion sensor like your inner ear. A person just wears them and lives: cooking, folding, tidying. The robot later relives the day through human eyes. The lovely realization: humanity performs billions of hours of hand-work daily — glasses can finally harvest it.

On Karan's website these are the three data products. "Stereo ego" you can now decode like an engineer: stereo = two eyes, ego = from the doer's point of view.
