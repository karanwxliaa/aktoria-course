Part 1 · Lesson 1

Recipes vs. learning

Two kinds of machines exist. Once you see the difference, everything else follows.

1. Machines that follow recipes

Your washing machine follows a recipe written once in a factory: soak, tumble, rinse, spin. Every wash, the exact same steps — and it never opens the lid to check if the clothes got clean. It follows the recipe; it never tastes the food.

A computer program is exactly that: a recipe card written by a person, followed by a very obedient cook with no judgment. The ATM, the microwave, the ticket machine — all recipe cards. For seventy years, all software worked this way.

2. Now try writing a recipe for something "easy"

Here's where the story turns. What recipe tells a computer: "Is there a dog in this photo?" Try it:

🎮 Write the dog rule

Tap a rule you'd write. I'll play the annoyingly honest computer.

Every rule fails — yet any four-year-old points and says "doggy!", even at a breed she's never seen. She never learned a rule. She was shown dogs until she simply knew.

You're the same: you'd spot your own child in any crowd, but you couldn't write down how. Some knowledge lives in experience, not words. No recipe card can hold it.

3. The big switch: show, don't tell

So the world stopped writing recipes and started showing examples — millions of labeled photos instead of rules, the way a child is shown dogs. Mistakes collapsed almost overnight. Every phone camera that finds faces today came from that switch.

This is machine learning: the machine works out its own recipe from many examples. It's behind ChatGPT, behind robots, and behind your son's company. The examples were the missing ingredient.

4. Watch a machine learn, live

What does "learning" physically look like? Inside the machine are billions of tiny tuning dials. Watch what showing examples does to them:

That's all "training" is: guess → check → nudge the dials → repeat, billions of times. It's how you perfected your own cooking — thirty years of pots, each one nudging the salt and timing a little. The machine just does it a million times an hour.

5. And a "model" is… a file

When training ends, the tuned dials get saved. That file is a model. Two strange things about it:

Nobody wrote it, nobody can read it. Even its makers can't point to where "the capital of France" is stored — the knowledge is smeared across all the dials, like your feel for dough is smeared across thirty years of hands.

It can be copied. Your kitchen instinct can't be handed to anyone. A model is practiced instinct that can be photocopied to a million machines overnight. Same robot arm, clumsy or brilliant — depending which model file is loaded.

2. The robots you've seen aren't what you think

Factory robots welding cars in sparks look intelligent. They're not — they're precise. One recorded motion, repeated a million times, blind. If the car arrived slightly out of place, they'd confidently weld the empty air. That's why they live behind safety cages: not dangerous minds, just blind muscles.

And your robot vacuum? It cleans like a blindfolded person sweeping: roll, bump, turn, repeat. Twenty years, millions sold — never picked up a single sock. None of these machines learn.

Part 2 · Lesson 4

Driving a robot from far away

The word Karan says most: teleoperation. It's a video call — where your hands come out the other side.

1. A video call with hands

On a video call you can see Karan's kitchen — but you can't stir his pot. Now imagine a robot standing there. You see through its camera eyes, you move your hands — its hands copy you, the same instant. That's teleoperation: lending a robot your hands over the internet.

Two ways it's done: twin arms — you hold a small "leader" arm and the robot copies it, like guiding a child's hand over the pencil from another building. Or wearing the robot — a headset shows its view, controllers carry your movements. The puppeteer, moved inside the puppet.

2. Older and more trusted than you think

Invented in the 1940s so scientists could handle radioactive material through thick glass — a person moved two handles, metal tongs copied every motion on the dangerous side.

And today's "robot surgery"? The surgeon moves finger controls; robot arms turn them into tiny, perfectly steady movements inside the patient. Every motion is the surgeon's. Millions of operations a year — and the robot even smooths away the natural tremble of the human hand.

📖 A true story — surgery across an ocean

In 2001, a surgeon in New York operated on a patient lying in France. His hand movements crossed the Atlantic; the video came back. 45 minutes; she recovered perfectly. The round-trip delay: about one blink of an eye. Remember that — it's about to matter.

3. The enemy: delay — feel it yourself

Karan's whole engineering battle in one word: delay — the time between your movement and seeing the robot respond. You've felt it on video calls ("no no, you go first"). Annoying in talk. Deadly for hands.

1. The open secret

Karan's company introduces itself with one cheeky sentence: "Every 'autonomous' robot you've seen this year had a human behind it." They mean it literally — if a robot online did something delicate and surprising, a person was probably driving it, from the next room or the next continent.

📖 A true story — the $20,000 house robot

Last year a company started selling the first humanoid home robot — a friendly machine in a knitted suit, $20,000. A famous journalist tested one. Then came the discovery: everything it did was driven, live, by an employee in a headset in another building. The internet laughed — "$20,000 to let a stranger wander my house!"

The company never denied it. That is the plan: the drivers are the robot's teachers. Every session is recorded and teaches it to do the chore alone next time. Early buyers are hiring an apprentice — plus the tutor.

3. The two rules of good lessons

Variety. A cook who's only cooked in her own kitchen panics in her sister's; a cook of fifty kitchens cooks anywhere. So collectors keep moving the cup, changing the tablecloth, dimming the lights — fifty slightly different lessons teach a robot to cope, not just repeat.

Quality. A photocopier copies the smudges along with the letters. If the demonstrator hesitates or fumbles, the robot faithfully learns to hesitate and fumble. Measured: 200 lessons from one calm expert beat 300 from six careless helpers. Now you know why Aktoria insists on trained, in-house operators — the robot photocopies its teacher.
